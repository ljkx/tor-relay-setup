Dear [PROVIDER] abuse team,

Thank you for forwarding the notice from [COPYRIGHT CLAIMANT] about
[WORK / FILE NAME], reported for [IP ADDRESS] at [TIME AND TIME ZONE].

[IP ADDRESS] runs a Tor exit relay, [NICKNAME]. Tor is a free, open network
that thousands of volunteers operate so people can use the Internet
privately and safely. The relay passes encrypted traffic on behalf of Tor
users; the traffic in the notice did not start on this server, and the
material is not stored or published here.

As the operator I do not choose, change or inspect what passes through the
relay, and I keep no logs that could identify the person behind a
connection. I therefore cannot identify, warn or block an individual user,
and I have no copy of the material to take down.

# US providers: the next paragraph is the core of the EFF template.
In the United States this kind of transmission falls under the "mere
conduit" safe harbor of the DMCA, 17 U.S.C. § 512(a), not the
notice-and-takedown process of § 512(c), which applies to material stored on
a server. Courts have held that § 512(c) notices do not apply to conduits
(RIAA v. Verizon, 351 F.3d 1229 (D.C. Cir. 2003); In re Charter
Communications, 393 F.3d 771 (8th Cir. 2005)). [PROVIDER] keeps its own safe
harbor as long as it maintains a repeat-infringer policy; no further action
is needed for this notice.

You can confirm that [IP ADDRESS] was a Tor exit at the reported time with
the Tor Project's ExoneraTor service:
https://metrics.torproject.org/exonerator.html?ip=[IP ADDRESS]&timestamp=[YYYY-MM-DD]

To lower the chance of file sharing through this relay, it uses Tor's
reduced exit policy, which blocks most ports used by peer-to-peer software.
